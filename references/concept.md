# Concept Archetype

Research framework for theories, ideas, methodologies, philosophies, scientific fields, ideologies, intellectual movements, abstract principles, or any subject that exists primarily in the domain of knowledge and thought.

## When to Use

The subject is an idea or body of knowledge rather than a physical thing, person, organization, or event. "String theory", "stoicism", "blockchain consensus mechanisms", "behavioral economics", "the Fermi paradox", "critical race theory", "agile methodology", "general relativity" all map here.

Key distinction from Artifact: if the subject is a specific implementation (Bitcoin, the iPhone), use Artifact. If it's the underlying idea or field (cryptocurrency consensus, smartphone design philosophy), use Concept.

## Research Dimensions

These dimensions define what to investigate and ensure adequate coverage of. They are **not** chapter headings — the report's TOC should be crafted fresh for each subject with unique, compelling titles. A dimension can span multiple chapters, be woven into other sections, or be combined with another dimension. The goal is coverage, not section-matching.

### One-Line Definition

What is this concept? For concepts, the definition deserves slightly more space because the subject itself is abstract. Write it so a smart non-expert can grasp the core idea. One paragraph, no jargon without explanation.

### Genesis

Every idea has an origin story. Where did this come from?

- **The problem or question**: What intellectual puzzle, practical need, or dissatisfaction with existing ideas gave rise to this concept?
- **Precursors**: What earlier ideas laid the groundwork? What was the intellectual soil this grew from?
- **The breakthrough**: Who formulated it, when, and how? What was the key insight? What made it different from what came before?
- **Context**: What was happening in the field/world at the time? What made this moment ripe for this idea?
- **Initial reception**: How was it received? Embraced immediately? Ignored? Attacked? By whom?

For scientific concepts: focus on the original paper(s), the key experiment(s), and the intellectual lineage. Cite specific works.

For philosophical/ideological concepts: focus on the thinker(s), their influences, and the intellectual tradition they were responding to.

### Core Content

The meat of the concept — what it actually says, claims, or proposes. This is the "How It Works" equivalent for ideas.

- **The central claim or framework**: State the core idea clearly and precisely. If there are formal definitions, include them. If there are equations or models, explain them in plain language.
- **Key components**: Break the concept into its constituent parts. What are the building blocks?
- **How it works**: Walk through the logic, the mechanism, or the argument. The reader should understand the concept well enough to explain it to someone else.
- **Assumptions**: What does this concept take for granted? What must be true for it to hold?
- **Scope**: Where does this concept apply? Where does it stop applying?

Write this section at the level of a good university lecture — rigorous but accessible. Don't assume prior expertise, but don't condescend. Use examples to ground abstract ideas.

### Evolution and Development

How the concept has changed, grown, or been refined since its origin.

- **Key developments**: Major refinements, extensions, or revisions. Who contributed them?
- **Branching**: Did the concept split into schools of thought or competing versions?
- **Integration**: How has the concept been adopted or adapted by other fields?
- **Empirical status**: For scientific concepts — what evidence has accumulated for and against? What experiments or observations have been key?
- **Practical applications**: How has the concept been applied in the real world? With what results?

### Debates and Critiques

No concept exists without challenge. What are the arguments against it?

- **The strongest critiques**: What are the most serious objections? Present them fairly and specifically.
- **Competing frameworks**: What alternative concepts or theories address the same questions? Where do they agree and disagree?
- **Open questions**: What does this concept not yet explain? Where are the known gaps?
- **Controversies**: Are there active disputes? What are the fault lines?

Present critiques on their merits — don't dismiss them to defend the concept, and don't amplify them to seem balanced. Just lay out the genuine state of the debate.

### State of Play

Where this concept stands now in its field.

- Current status: mainstream? fringe? emerging? declining?
- Active research frontiers (for scientific concepts)
- Current relevance and influence
- Recent developments or renewed interest
- How practitioners/experts currently view it

### Verdict

Analytical assessment — but note that for concepts, "verdict" is less about future prediction and more about intellectual evaluation.

- **What it gets right**: Where is this concept genuinely powerful, illuminating, or useful?
- **Where it falls short**: What are its real limitations — not theoretical objections but practical or explanatory gaps?
- **Significance**: How has this concept changed its field or the broader intellectual landscape?
- **Durability**: Will this concept endure, evolve, or be superseded? What would it take to overthrow it?
- **Why it matters**: For the non-specialist reader — why should anyone care about this concept? What does understanding it give you?

## Coverage priority

Spend the most space on the evidence that explains the subject and changes the reader’s judgment. Assign research ownership dynamically from the coverage map; do not force a fixed agent split.

## Coverage ledger mapping

Turn every relevant dimension above into one or more ledger coverage items with
an explicit priority, decision relevance, completion criterion, and evidence
target. If a dimension is relevant but cannot be resolved, keep it in the
ledger as `status: gap` with a concrete `gap_impact`; never omit it silently.
