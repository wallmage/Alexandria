# Evidence-safe English edit

Edit for a clear, direct, human voice.

- Lead with the point.
- Give each section a job: answer a reader question, explain the evidence or mechanism, test a material limit, and state the consequence.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Cut throat-clearing, repetition, and generic transitions.
- Vary sentence length when the idea warrants it.
- Replace slogans and vague praise with observable facts.
- Keep technical language when it is precise; explain it once.
- Use headings that tell the reader something.
- Avoid canned openings, forced contrasts, fake quotations, and ornamental metaphors.
- Do not turn uncertainty into certainty.
- Do not add facts, examples, or color that the evidence ledger does not support.
- Use concrete cases or worked examples where they materially improve understanding; do not add decorative anecdotes.
- Preserve the report's hard length range by replacing repetition with deeper evidence, mechanisms, counterevidence, comparisons, or implications.
- State a decision once at the executive level and once where the evidence is
  synthesized. Merge buyer profiles, checklists, and pilot instructions when
  they ask the reader to evaluate the same dimensions.
- If a number is material enough to invoke, state it and cite it. Do not tell
  the reader that a published range exists while withholding the range.
- Define product labels and technical terms at first use. Remove names that do
  not change the reader's understanding or decision.
- Put a citation in every paragraph that makes a platform-specific, numerical,
  legal, policy, or other externally verifiable claim. A bibliography entry
  alone does not make nearby prose traceable.

## Measurable caps

Style advice that cannot be counted does not survive a long draft. Count these
on the finished report, excluding headings, tables, block quotes, and the
Sources section. Every figure is a ceiling. Coming in far under it is a good
result, not a missed target.

| Pattern | Cap |
|---|---|
| Sentences carrying five or more comma-separated coordinate items | 3 per 1,000 words |
| `X is not Y. It is Z.` frame | 2 per report |
| Semicolon antithesis (`for A it is X; for B it is Y`) | 2 per report |
| Formulaic paragraph closers (the four templates below, combined) | 20% of body paragraphs |
| `, which is` / `, which means` / `, which makes` / `, which tells` appositive verdicts | 1 per 1,000 words |
| Em dashes | 2 per 1,000 words |
| Antithetical contrast in any form: `rather than`, `not X but Y`, `A, not B` | 1 per 500 words |
| Self-reference: `this report`, `this analysis`, `these findings` | 2 per report |
| Hedge stacks (two or more hedges qualifying one claim: `may potentially suggest`) | 0 |

Where a cap collides with accuracy, accuracy wins. Restore the qualifier, the
citation, or the contrast the evidence actually requires and cut the decorative
instances elsewhere.

### The four formulaic closers

Name them so a reviewer can find them:

1. **Comma antithesis.** `The right read is more conservative, not less.`
2. **Colon verdict.** `It redirects evaluation effort: how well an agent fits now matters more than what it scored last quarter.`
3. **`, which` appositive verdict.** `Seats are billed monthly, which tells a budget owner that the seat price is not the cost.`
4. **Semicolon antithesis.** `For a hiring manager the distinction is decisive; for an architect, it is noise.`

Each is a competent sentence. The defect is repetition. When one in every two
paragraphs lands on the same shape, the reader stops reading the judgment and
starts hearing the meter, and a report that sounds this consistent stops
sounding like a person wrote it.

### Let paragraphs end

At least half of body paragraphs should end on their last fact, figure, source,
or mechanical explanation, with no evaluative clause after it. A paragraph that
has done its work does not need a verdict attached; the reader can carry an
unresolved fact into the next paragraph, and a report that trusts them to do so
reads as confident rather than anxious.

Reserve the closing judgment for section ends and for the two or three moments
where the implication is genuinely the point. Do not add an epigram to
manufacture weight.

## De-hedging

Hedges belong in a report. Hedge placement is what makes them read as either
precision or retreat.

- **Front-load the caveat.** Put the qualifier at the start of the sentence so
  the paragraph lands on substance: `Within the vendor's own benchmark, latency
  fell by a third` rather than `Latency fell by a third, though this comes from
  the vendor's own benchmark and may not generalize.` Both sentences carry the
  same uncertainty; only one leaves the reader holding a fact.
- **One hedge per claim.** Choose the hedge that describes the actual weakness
  in the evidence. Stacking two is not extra caution, and a reader reads it as
  the writer having no position.
- **Attribute instead of hedging** when the uncertainty is really about who
  said it. `The filing reports 12% churn` beats `churn may be around 12%`.
- **Name the thesis twice.** State the governing judgment at the executive
  level and again where the evidence is synthesized. Repeating its keyword in
  every section reads as insecurity about whether the argument landed, and it
  crowds out the section's own contribution.
- **Keep the report out of the report.** Describe the subject, not the
  document. `This report finds that adoption stalled in 2024` is a sentence
  about the writing; `Adoption stalled in 2024` is a sentence about the world.

## Reading passes

Read the draft once for the argument, once for value density, once for evidence
fidelity, and once aloud for rhythm. The rhythm pass is where the caps above
get caught; run a search for the four closers and for the em dash before
declaring it done.

If a smooth rewrite changes a number, attribution, qualifier, citation, or
causal claim, restore the verified meaning.
